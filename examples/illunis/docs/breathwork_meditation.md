# Breathwork and Meditation: Evidence Review and First-Release Session Proposal

Status: research note, 2026-10-28. No code changes.
Scope: holotropic breathing, pranayama, breath pacing, breath retention,
meditation techniques, safety screens, a proposed first-release session set,
and pacing rules for a text-and-visual guided session.

## How to read the evidence labels

Each factual claim carries one of these labels:

- **Strong**: systematic review or meta-analysis of randomized trials, with
  the caveats the authors stated.
- **Moderate**: meta-analysis or several randomized trials, with high
  heterogeneity or risk of bias.
- **Weak**: one or two small trials, non-randomized studies, or only
  narrative or hypothesis papers.
- **Practitioner**: teaching manuals, facilitator guidance, commercial
  breathwork pages, or forum advice. No outcome data.

Citations use [S#] and point to the Sources list at the end. Only pages read
in full, or with a clear abstract or summary in the search results, are cited.
Where a page could not be read, the claim is marked **not verified** or left
out.

Several sources were read only as search-result snippets. Those are marked
"(snippet)" in the text so the reader knows the claim was not checked against
the full paper.

## 1. Holotropic breathing (Stanislav and Christina Grof)

### 1.1 The method as taught

The Grofs' 1990 principles document is the most direct primary text found.
It is a short statement of theory and practice, not a clinical protocol [S1].

- Basic elements: "deeper and accelerated breathing, evocative music, and
  facilitation of energy release through a specific form of bodywork," with
  "mandala" drawing and discussion afterward [S1].
- Format: one-to-one, or preferably in a group where participants alternate
  as "experiencers" and "Sitters" [S1].
- Breathing: "Holotropic Breathing is faster and deeper than usual; generally,
  no other specific instructions are given before or during the session as to
  the rate, pattern, and nature of breathing" [S1].
- Sitter role: "responsive and non-intrusive," ensure effective breathing,
  "create a safe environment," and provide physical help [S1].
- Bodywork: energy-release bodywork at the end, which the principles describe
  as "an essential part of the Holotropic approach" [S1].
- Discussion: a separate discussion period on the same day, in which the
  facilitator "does not give interpretations of the material, based on a
  specific theoretical system" [S1].
- Ethics: the principles describe possible "unusually intense projections" onto
  the facilitator and an "imbalance of power" to be handled with care [S1].
- Naming: if a practice "departs significantly" from these descriptions, the
  name should not be used [S1].

### 1.2 Session length

- The principles say: "It is important to leave sufficient time for the
  sessions, usually between two and three hours." They add that the process
  "is allowed to reach a natural closure; in exceptional cases, this can take a
  few hours" [S1].
- Third-party breathwork pages repeat the two-to-three-hour range [S2].
- Strength: **Practitioner** (the Grofs' own teaching document, no outcome data).

### 1.3 Music

- The principles describe a characteristic arc: "at the beginning, it is
  evocative and stimulating, later it becomes increasingly dramatic and
  dynamic, and finally it reaches a breakthrough quality," then shifts to
  "quieter music" and ends with "peaceful, flowing, and meditative selections"
  [S1].
- The principles also say the arc "should be modified if the energy in the
  group suggests that a different pattern is indicated" [S1].
- Music is "an integral part of the Holotropic process" [S1].
- Strength: **Practitioner**.

### 1.4 Facilitator training

- The Institute for Holotropics / Grof Transpersonal Training (GTT) materials
  state that a certification pathway exists and is built from modules
  (snippet) [S3].
- A third-party source gives a 600-hour course figure. **Not verified** against
  an official page, so it is not used here.
- The GTT info pack refers to a minimum training period of two years (snippet)
  [S3]. **Not verified** in full text.

### 1.5 Reported risks

Sources and what they say:

- **Hyperventilation and its physical effects.** The principles themselves do
  not list physical risks. They say physical and emotional contraindications
  "are discussed and if there are any concerns, expert assessment is obtained"
  [S1]. Practitioner sources state that prolonged hyperventilation can cause
  "fainting, muscle spasms severe enough to be painful, and (rarely) seizures
  in susceptible individuals" [S2]. Strength: **Practitioner**.
- **Tetany.** Practitioner sources describe hand and mouth cramping as
  "tetany" or "lobster claw hands" and call it benign and reversible in healthy
  adults [S2][S4]. The physiology in the clinical literature is: low CO2
  (hypocapnia) causes respiratory alkalosis, which lowers ionized calcium and
  raises neuromuscular excitability [S5][S6]. Strength for the mechanism:
  **Moderate** (clinical overviews; **not read in full**).
- **Seizures.** Seizure history is one of the most common exclusions across
  practitioner forms [S4][S7]. No source found quantifies the risk. Strength:
  **Practitioner**.
- **Cardiac and blood-pressure effects.** Listed exclusions include heart
  disease, angina, heart attack, and uncontrolled high blood pressure [S4][S7].
  Strength: **Practitioner**.
- **Glaucoma and retinal detachment.** Practitioner forms list these, on the
  reasoning that raised pressure may aggravate them [S7]. Strength:
  **Practitioner**.
- **Psychological distress.** Practitioner sources list psychosis, bipolar I,
  schizophrenia, active suicidal ideation, and hospitalization for psychiatric
  reasons within the past 10 years as exclusions [S7]. The Grofs' principles
  warn of intense projections [S1]. Strength: **Practitioner**.
- **Pregnancy.** Several practitioner forms exclude pregnancy, and some say
  pregnant participants should get primary-care approval first [S4][S7].
  Strength: **Practitioner**.
- **Aneurysm, recent surgery or injury, osteoporosis, prescription blood
  thinners.** Listed by practitioner forms [S4][S7]. Strength: **Practitioner**.

Note on cardiac events. No source found in the search results reports cardiac
events as an outcome of holotropic sessions. The claim appears only as an
exclusion list item. This is a gap, not evidence of safety.

### 1.6 Who the sources say should not do it

Practitioner exclusion lists overlap heavily. Most are from booking forms and
breathwork business pages, not from the Grof organization [S4][S7]. The
search did not surface an official Grof Transpersonal Training contraindication
list. Confirm current criteria with the training organization if precision
matters.

### 1.7 Evidence base

- A 2007 hypothesis paper by Rhinewine and Williams proposed holotropic
  breathing as a possible treatment for anxiety and depression. The authors
  framed it as a hypothesis, citing preliminary evidence [S8]. Strength:
  **Weak** (hypothesis paper; **not read in full**, only identified in search).
- No systematic review or RCT focused specifically on holotropic breathing was
  found. Searches for one returned only practitioner pages and the hypothesis
  paper above.

**Overall: the evidence base for holotropic breathing is thin and mostly
practitioner-generated.** Its safety lists come from practitioners. No
controlled safety data was found.

### 1.8 Decision for this app

Holotropic breathing is excluded from the default library. Reasons:

- Session length of two to three hours plus discussion and bodywork cannot fit
  an app session [S1].
- Sessions require a trained facilitator and a Sitter, and they are group or
  one-to-one [S1].
- The method is designed to produce "non-ordinary states," and the principles
  call the experience "largely nonverbal, without interventions" [S1]. This
  conflicts with a wellbeing app's need to check in during and after a session.
- Practitioner exclusion lists are long, and the app cannot screen reliably by
  text [S4][S7].
- Evidence is thin [S1][S8].

## 2. Pranayama

### 2.1 Techniques and what the research says

Evidence labels below are for each technique, not for the tradition.

**Nadi shodhana (alternate nostril breathing)**

- Systematic review of 44 RCTs (Ghiya, 2016) on autonomic, cardiopulmonary,
  and cognitive outcomes. Risk of bias: ten high, twenty-nine low, five unknown.
  Most participants were healthy, and the technique is not standardized [S11].
  The authors call the autonomic and cardiopulmonary evidence "high-level," but
  that label looks generous given the bias ratings and heterogeneity. Strength:
  **Weak to moderate**.
- 2024 meta-analysis of six RCTs (525 participants) on blood pressure: a mean
  systolic difference of about -7.16 mmHg, with I² = 93%, so the pooled value
  should be treated cautiously [S12]. Strength: **Weak** (very high
  heterogeneity, small trials, mostly healthy people).
- No reliable RCT evidence for nadi shodhana on anxiety was found in these
  results.

**Kapalabhati (skull-shining breath)**

- Forceful, rapid exhalations driven by the abdomen. Practitioner sources treat
  it as a forceful hyperventilation technique that can raise blood pressure
  and lower CO2 [S13].
- No RCT or systematic review of kapalabhati was found. Strength: **Practitioner**.

**Bhramari (humming bee breath)**

- A 2017 systematic review, as summarized in search results, found very low
  methodological quality and no RCTs [S14]. **Not verified in full.**
- A 2023 review, as summarized in search results, included six RCTs but mostly
  non-randomized trials, and reported positive effects on psychological,
  cardiovascular, and pulmonary measures [S14]. **Not verified in full.**
- Strength: **Weak**.

**Ujjayi (ocean breath, throat-constricted breath)**

- A 2012 study of 60 healthy men reported lower heart rate and blood pressure
  after six weeks of ujjayi with shavasana. The control group showed no change.
  HRV was not measured (snippet) [S15]. **Not verified in full.**
- An acute study in experienced practitioners found ujjayi produced less
  baroreflex improvement than other slow patterns, with no change in blood
  pressure (snippet) [S15]. **Not verified.**
- A registered RCT (CTRI/2025/01/079559, 80 participants, HRV as primary
  outcome, hypertension population) had no published results at the time of
  search [S16]. Strength: **Weak** (small or unpublished).

**Sama vritti (box breathing, equal-count breath)**

- See section 3.2 for the Balban 2023 RCT. Strength: **Weak** for mood effects
  (single RCT, remote delivery, small arms).

**Extended exhale (exhale longer than inhale)**

- A 12-week single-blind RCT of 100 healthy adults compared a slow breathing
  pattern with a longer exhale against equal inhale and exhale. Slow breathing
  for 12 weeks reduced PROMIS anxiety. The snippet did not show whether the
  longer-exhale arm beat the equal-ratio arm (snippet) [S17]. The match to this
  specific trial is **not confirmed**.
- The same snippet says acute paced breathing with a longer exhalation can
  increase vagal tone in healthy adults [S17]. **Not verified in full.**
- The authors (as summarized) say no study has documented a long-term
  difference in relaxation by breath ratio [S17]. Strength: **Weak**.

### 2.2 Breathing pace, HRV, and resonance frequency

**Slow breathing and HRV.** The strongest systematic review found is the
2022 review and meta-analysis by Laborde and colleagues in *Neuroscience &
Biobehavioral Reviews*. It pooled 223 studies on voluntary slow breathing and
vagally mediated HRV, at three timepoints: during breathing (172 studies),
right after one session (16), and after multi-session interventions (49). The
authors report higher vagally mediated HRV at all three timepoints. They also
suggest few expected adverse effects [S18].

- Effect sizes, heterogeneity, and risk of bias were not available from the
  abstract. These need the full text (DOI 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2022.104711) [S18].
- Strength: **Moderate** for an HRV association, based on the abstract alone.
  Causal claims about health outcomes are not supported by this review as
  summarized.

**Resonance-frequency breathing.** Resonance frequency (RF) breathing is
usually around 6 breaths per minute (0.1 Hz) [S19].

- Schwerdtfeger and colleagues (2020, *Clinical Neurophysiology*) argue that
  breathing at 6 breaths per minute may produce coherence of 0.1 Hz
  oscillations, linked to central pacemaker activity. The abstract is the
  part read; the full text was not [S19][S20].
- A 2024 systematic review of RF effects in slow-paced breathing (Vukojević
  and colleagues, as listed in search results) was not readable, so no findings
  are reported [S21].
- A 2023 Springer review describes three RF protocols: personal RF, a
  device-guided individual rate, and a preset pace (usually 6 bpm). It says the
  field lacks a gold-standard protocol [S22]. Journal name not confirmed from
  the snippet.
- A 2014 hypothesis-and-theory paper by Lehrer and Gevirtz reviews how HRV
  biofeedback may work. It names baroreflex homeostasis as the best-supported
  mechanism (abstract) [S23]. Strength: **Weak** (hypothesis).
- Individual RF can differ from 6 bpm, and a non-peer-reviewed podcast says
  some people are near 3.5 bpm [S24]. Treat this as low-quality support.

**Strength summary for pacing:**

- Slow breathing and HRV association: **Moderate** (systematic review,
  abstract only).
- 6 bpm as an effective default for mood or anxiety outcomes: **Weak**.
- Personal RF finding by app: **Weak** and requires device measurement.

### 2.3 Breath retention (kumbhaka)

No peer-reviewed systematic review of kumbhaka-specific adverse events was
found. Most results were wellness blogs, app pages, and a forum thread.

**Risks named in sources (all practitioner or low-grade):**

- **Shallow-water blackout.** Named as the most widely discussed hazard and
  described as potentially sudden, with no warning [S25]. Strength:
  **Practitioner** (blog). Note: this is from a commercial page. Shallow-water
  blackout is a known apnea hazard, but a peer-reviewed source was not
  retrieved in this session.
- **Hypoxia, hypercapnia, irregular heart rhythms, neurological effects.**
  Listed by an app reference list citing WebMD. **Not verified** because the
  cited page was not read [S26].
- **Neurological markers after prolonged voluntary apnea.** One article reports
  investigators calling for follow-up on repeated severe episodes. It describes
  the evidence as unsettled, not as proof of harm [S25]. Strength: **Weak**.

**Contraindications named by practitioners:** acute illness, epilepsy, severe
lung disease, terminal cancer, pacemakers or arrhythmias, pregnancy, and
tinnitus aggravation [S27]. Practitioner forum comments also say kumbhaka should
be done only after a solid pranayama background and never forcefully [S28].

**Pregnancy-specific practitioner advice.** Several prenatal yoga sources say
pregnant people should avoid breath holds and forceful breathing such as
bhastrika and kapalabhati [S29][S30]. No clinical guidance was found. Strength:
**Practitioner**.

**Interpretation for this app:** the only breath-hold type included in the
default library is a short, comfortable, unforced pause after a gentle exhale
(section 6). Retention after inhale, and any hold after forceful breathing, is
excluded.

## 3. Meditation techniques and evidence

### 3.1 Overall meditation evidence

- **Goyal et al. (2014), *JAMA Internal Medicine*.** Systematic review and
  meta-analysis of 47 randomized trials with active controls (3,515 adults),
  searched through November 2012 [S31].
  - Anxiety: moderate evidence of improvement, effect size 0.38 at 8 weeks and
    0.22 at 3 to 6 months [S31].
  - Depression: moderate evidence, effect size 0.30 at 8 weeks and 0.23 at 3
    to 6 months [S31].
  - Pain: moderate evidence, effect size 0.33 [S31].
  - Stress and distress, and mental health-related quality of life: low
    evidence of improvement [S31].
  - Positive mood, attention, substance use, sleep, and weight: low evidence of
    no effect, or insufficient evidence [S31].
  - "We found no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active
    treatment" [S31].
  - Strength: **Strong** for the listed outcomes, with the authors' own
    caveat that stronger designs are needed for positive mental health [S31].
- **Khoury et al. (2013), *Clinical Psychology Review*.** Meta-analysis of 209
  studies (12,145 participants). Hedges g was 0.55 for pre-post comparisons, 0.53
  for waitlist, and 0.33 for active treatments [S32]. The authors said MBT was
  "especially effective for reducing anxiety, depression, and stress" [S32].
  A later conference poster by the same group reports a different version (166
  publications, pre-post g = .60). Cite the journal version, not the poster
  [S32]. Strength: **Moderate** (heterogeneous, much pre-post and waitlist data).

Both reviews are for mindfulness-based programs as a whole. They do not show
which individual techniques drive the effect.

### 3.2 Technique-level evidence

**Focused attention (FA) and open monitoring (OM).** Lutz, Slagter, Dunne, and
Davidson (2008, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) separate two styles. In FA,
attention is aimed at a chosen target. In OM, practitioners track whatever
arises without reacting [S33]. The review also notes that earlier neuroscience
work on meditation had limited statistical power, weak control groups, and
inconsistent definitions [S33]. FA is usually the starting point for novices
[S34].

- Strength: **Conceptual** (framework paper). The brain-region findings were not
  read in full.
- No pooled effect sizes for FA or OM as separate techniques were found in this
  search. Strength: **Weak** for technique-specific claims.

**Body scan.**

- Gan, Zhang and Chen (2022) meta-analysis of body-scan RCTs: 14 studies. Body
  scan had a small effect on mindfulness (Hedges g = .268, 95% CI 0.032 to
  0.504) compared with passive control. Other outcomes had no pooled effect
  reported. The authors conclude "a sole body scan meditation is not effective
  enough to improve health-related outcomes." Risk of bias was low, and
  heterogeneity high [S35]. Strength: **Moderate** for the mindfulness effect,
  **Weak** for health outcomes.
- A 2014 RCT (55 outpatients with chronic pain) found a 10-minute body scan
  reduced pain-related distress in clinic, but not in everyday settings. The
  authors say benefits need confirmation in the field (abstract) [S36]. Strength:
  **Weak**.
- The OPTIMUM trial (MBSR-based telehealth for chronic low back pain, planned
  n ≈ 450) had no published outcomes at search time [S38]. Strength: **None yet**.

**Loving-kindness meditation (LKM).**

- Zeng et al. (2015, *Frontiers in Psychology*): 24 empirical articles, combined
  N = 1,759 [S39].
  - Daily positive emotions in waitlist-controlled RCTs: g = 0.395 (95% CI 0.258
    to 0.533) for high-arousal positive emotions, g = 0.392 (0.254 to 0.530) for
    low-arousal positive emotions. Described as medium [S39].
  - Non-RCT studies: g about 0.29 to 0.32, with one CI including zero [S39].
  - During practice versus memory training: g = 0.929 (large) [S39].
  - LKM-focused interventions: g = 0.424 (medium). Compassion-focused: about 0.27
    to 0.29, with CIs including zero. The subgroup difference was not significant
    [S39].
  - The authors call the conclusions "rather exploratory" and say 38.9% of
    studies were high risk of bias [S39].
  - Strength: **Moderate** for daily positive emotion in waitlist RCTs.
- A 2021 Galante or 2017 Hilton meta-analysis on loving-kindness was not found
  in search, so none is cited.

**Mindful breath counting and short practices.** Found no separate systematic
review. The Balban 2023 mindfulness control (5 minutes, focusing between the
eyes) is the only short-session mindfulness comparator in these results [S41].

**Open-ended noting and sound-based practices.** No systematic review found in
this search. Strength: **Weak / not established**.

### 3.3 MBSR structure (for reference)

Multiple providers describe the same format [S42][S43][S44]:

- Eight weekly group classes, about 2.5 to 3 hours each, plus an optional
  orientation.
- One long retreat day, usually between weeks 6 and 7, of about 6 to 7.5 hours
  (one commercial page gives 8 hours [S43]).
- Home practice of about 45 minutes per day, guided by audio recordings [S42][S44].
- Content: body scan, mindful movement or gentle yoga, sitting meditation,
  later topics including stress reactivity and communication [S42][S44].

These are provider descriptions, not the original Kabat-Zinn protocol text,
which was not retrieved. **Strength: Practitioner** for exact parameters.

### 3.4 Adverse effects of meditation (applies to all techniques)

This is the most important safety finding for a meditation feature.

- Farias et al. (2020, *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*): systematic review of
  83 studies (6,703 adults). Searches ran to October 2019. Excluded physical
  practices such as yoga postures [S45].
  - 55 of 83 studies (65%) reported at least one meditation-related adverse
    event [S45].
  - Pooled prevalence 8.3%, but 3.7% in experimental studies and 33.2% in
    observational studies [S45].
  - Most common: anxiety (33% of studies), depression (27%), cognitive anomalies
    (25%). Gastrointestinal problems and suicidal behaviour were least common,
    at 11% each [S45].
  - Authors: events "are not rare" and occur in people with no earlier mental
    health problems [S45].
  - Strength: **Moderate** (high heterogeneity, as the authors note).
- Goldberg et al. (2021, *Psychotherapy Research*): population-based US sample.
  Authors conclude that meditation-related adverse effects are common, even with
  modest practice [S46].
- Britton et al. (2021, *Clinical Psychological Science*): a measurement paper
  that proposes definitions and a 44-item interview for meditation-related
  adverse effects, applying 24 harms-monitoring recommendations to an eight-week
  program [S47].
- Lindahl et al. (2017, *PLOS ONE*), qualitative study of Western Buddhist
  meditators: sample was self-selected, mostly teachers, nearly all white and
  highly educated. Not designed to measure prevalence. Some effects were
  described as serious and long-lasting. Social support was a common remedy
  [S48]. Strength: **Weak** for prevalence, **Moderate** for the taxonomy as a
  starting point.
- Van Gordon, Shonin and García-Campayo (2017) editorial: "Are there adverse
  effects associated with mindfulness?" [S49]. The indexed record does not show
  its recommendations. **Not verified in full.**
- A 2020 editorial argues that concern about mindfulness worsening psychosis is
  partly a myth, while noting that concern persists and that psychotherapy
  harms are studied less systematically than drug harms [S50]. Strength:
  **Practitioner / commentary**.

## 5. Proposed first-release session set (8 sessions)

Goals: prefer strongest evidence and lowest risk. Use paced breathing,
attention, and short mindfulness practices. Exclude high-intensity breathing
and long retention from the default library. Every session carries the
"not medical advice" line and a stop rule (section 6).

Default screens for all sessions: no session should be started by a person who
reports, in the app's own intake, one of: pregnancy, seizure disorder, heart
rhythm condition, uncontrolled hypertension, or recent psychosis or mania. The
app should show a "talk to a clinician first" notice rather than run the session.
(This follows the practitioner screen pattern and is a design choice, not a
guideline. Phrase the notice without diagnosing.)

| # | Name | Technique | Duration | Timing cues | Screens that apply |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Breath as it is | Observation of natural breath, no control | 3 min | Breath counts only after first minute | Default |
| 2 | Paced breath | Slow breathing near 6 breaths per minute | 5 min | 5 s in, 5 s out | Default; RF notice (below) |
| 3 | Long exhale | Inhale 4 s, exhale 6 to 8 s | 5 min | No holds | Default; stop if dizzy |
| 4 | Cyclic sighing | Two inhales through nose, long exhale through mouth | 5 min | Repeat pattern, no holds | Default |
| 5 | Box, fixed count | Equal count (4 s) for in, hold, out, hold | 5 min | Holds only if comfortable; no breath-hold test | Default; hold rule below |
| 6 | Body scan | Attention moves through body parts | 10 min | Lying or seated, with eyes open option | Default |
| 7 | Focused attention on breath | Count breaths 1 to 10, restart on losing count | 10 min | Silent counting, visual dot | Default |
| 8 | Loving-kindness | Repeat phrases toward self, then a friend | 10 min | Phrases on screen, pauses between | Default; check-in after; stop if distress |

Open-monitoring (noting sounds or thoughts) is held for a second release because
no systematic review was found for it as a separate technique (section 3.2).

### 5.1 Why these eight

- Session 2 and 3 use paced breathing, which has the strongest breathing-specific
  evidence in this review (section 2.2).
- Sessions 4 and 5 are included because the one RCT that tested them (Balban 2023)
  shows a mood effect versus a mindfulness control, but the effect is from one
  remote study with small arms [S41]. Strength: **Weak**. Include as
  experimental, label accordingly.
- Session 6 and 7 use the best-supported meditation mechanism (attention) with
  weak but consistent body-scan evidence [S35][S36].
- Session 8 uses LKM, which has moderate evidence for positive emotion in
  waitlist RCTs [S39].

### 5.2 Excluded from the default library

- **Holotropic breathing.** Excluded. Reasons: 2 to 3 hour format, facilitator
  and Sitter required, deliberate deep fast breathing, long contraindication
  lists, intense non-ordinary states, and no controlled safety or efficacy data
  [S1][S4][S7][S8]. Not appropriate for an app with no facilitator.
- **Kapalabhati, bhastrika, breath of fire.** Excluded. Forceful
  hyperventilation with practitioner warnings for hypertension, epilepsy,
  pregnancy, and recent abdominal surgery [S13][S29].
- **Kumbhaka (breath retention) beyond a short, comfortable pause.** Excluded.
  No systematic safety review, and practitioner warnings [S25][S27][S28].
- **Cyclic hyperventilation with retention** (the third Balban arm). Excluded.
  It combines deep rapid breathing with a 15-second empty-lung hold [S41].
- **Nadi shodhana, bhramari, ujjayi.** Deferred, not excluded. Nadi shodhana has
  the most data among pranayama techniques, but the evidence is weak and
  heterogeneous (section 2.1). Bhramari and ujjayi evidence is smaller still.
  Add later with a caveat, if at all.

### 5.3 Session scripts (outline, not final text)

Each session: a single line of intent, the pacing rule, a count-in, and an end
prompt. No medical claims in copy. For example, session 2 says "Slow your
breath and notice how it feels," not "lowers blood pressure" or "improves
heart rate variability."

## 6. Pacing for a text-and-visual guided session

No voice. The app uses text, a breathing visual (expanding and contracting
shape or a line), and a timer.

### 6.1 Timing rules

- **Inhale and exhale durations.** Paced breath: 5 s in, 5 s out (about 6
  breaths per minute) [S19][S20]. Long exhale: 4 s in, 6 to 8 s out [S17].
  Cyclic sighing: short inhale, short top-up inhale, long exhale; exact
  seconds were not in the read text (Balban protocol described as "slow inhale
  ... inhale again once more ... slowly and fully exhale") [S41]. Use a team
  choice, and verify the duration against the full paper before publication.
- **Box.** Equal counts of 4 s for in, hold, out, hold [S41]. The Balban
  protocol adapts counts with a CO2 tolerance test. **Do not use that test in
  the app.** It means breath-holding to the point of discomfort. Use a fixed
  count instead.
- **Hold rules (proposed; not from a guideline).**
  - Holds are only after a gentle exhale, never after a deep or fast inhale.
  - Maximum hold of 4 s in the default library.
  - No empty-lung holds (the Balban arm used 15 s; exclude).
  - Any hold can be skipped. The visual continues without a hold.
- **Rate.** Breath visual never goes faster than 6 breaths per minute in the
  default library.
- **Count-in.** Three slow cycles before the timer starts, shown as text.
- **Timer.** Session length shown, with a pause button that does not reset.

### 6.2 Visual pacing

- Breath visual expands over the inhale and contracts over the exhale. For box,
  the visual holds its state during a hold (no movement).
- Colour and motion must respect reduced-motion settings. Provide a
  non-animated option (a text countdown).
- Avoid flashing or high-contrast pulsing.

### 6.3 Stop and safety prompts

Stop prompts from practitioner sources: dizziness, lightheadedness,
tingling or numbness in hands or face, chest tightness, visual disturbance
[S13]. Include these as on-screen text with a "Stop" button that ends the
session and shows a neutral message.

Add a check-in at each session end (section 6.4). If a user reports distress,
the app ends the session and shows the "not medical advice" line with a
pointer to a qualified professional. Do not diagnose.

### 6.4 End-of-session check-in

- Three items, each on a 0 to 10 slider or three buttons:
  - How calm do you feel now? (compare to a pre-session item)
  - How much tension is in your body now?
  - Anything uncomfortable? (yes or no; if yes, show a stop message)
- Pre-session item: same three items, before the session starts. Use the
  difference, not an absolute "effect" claim.
- Log locally. Do not claim the check-in measures health outcomes.

### 6.5 Copy rules

- Use "may help you notice," "many people find," and "you can stop at any time."
- Avoid: "lowers blood pressure," "improves HRV," "treats anxiety," "heals," "resets
  your nervous system."
- Always include: "This is not medical advice. If you have a health condition or
  feel unwell, speak to a qualified clinician before you practise."

## 7. Gaps and next steps

- Holotropic: no official Grof safety document beyond the 1990 principles
  was retrieved. The principles do not list contraindications [S1].
- Laborde 2022: abstract only. Effect sizes and risk-of-bias detail need the
  full text [S18].
- Balban 2023: one remote RCT, mostly mood and respiratory-rate outcomes,
  with HRV and resting HR showing no change [S41]. Full text needed for exact
  durations.
- Breath retention: no peer-reviewed safety review found.
- Eating-disorder screen: not searched.
- Clinical screening guidance (pregnancy, cardiovascular, seizures) from a
  medical body was not located. Practitioner lists only.
- Several primary papers (Rhinewine and Williams 2007, Lehrer and Gevirtz 2014
  full text, the 2024 pranayama mental-disorder review full text) were read
  only as search summaries.
- Whether a validated breathwork-adverse-effects questionnaire exists was not
  established.
